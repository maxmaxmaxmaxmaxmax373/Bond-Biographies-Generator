Synthesis: What the Prices Say#

1. Object of this chapter#

The forty-nine biographies in this book each study one security. This chapter studies the collection. It assembles regularities that recur across the biographies and states what the assembled price and quantity records do and do not establish. The evidence is the Hall–Payne–Sargent database: monthly prices and quantities outstanding for U.S. federal securities from 1790 to 1960, together with the statutory terms of each issue. The method throughout has been to treat a bond price as a measurement — an opinion, recorded at a date, about whether a government would keep a particular promise — and to ask what moved the measurements.

Six findings organize the chapter. (i) A price cannot be read until its unit of account is identified; for roughly a quarter of the sample the unit is not what it appears to be. (ii) Several of the largest issues in the collection were priced by regulation rather than by time preference, and one deliberate design change in 1909 lets us measure the difference. (iii) Much of what looks like price drift is the mechanical arithmetic of embedded options and finite maturities, not news about credit. (iv) The terms on which the Treasury could sell debt — primary-market prices and coupons — measure reputation more sharply than secondary-market prices do. (v) The two-century record is a repeated cycle: credit destroyed by war or panic, then rebuilt by a fiscal settlement that the market learns to believe; the cycle ran intact until 1933, when the government for the first time repriced an outstanding promise by statute. (vi) The gaps and silences in the record are themselves data.

2. Units of account#

A dollar price of a dollar claim is uninformative until one knows which dollar is meant. Three episodes in the collection turn on this point.

First, the Civil War. After specie payments were suspended in December 1861, the quotations in the database are greenback prices of bonds whose coupons — and, after the Public Credit Act of 1869, principal — were payable in coin. The Loan of February 1861 traded at 107–114 in 1864 while the gold premium exceeded 100 percent; in gold units the bond stood near 50. The biographies of the wartime issues (the February 1861 loan, the Five-Twenties, the Ten-Forties) therefore read every wartime movement twice: once as a statement about Union credit, once as a statement about the greenback. Most of the drama belongs to the greenback. The March 1865 fall in quoted prices, which a naive reading assigns to a credit event at the moment of Union victory, is the gold premium collapsing.

Second, the War of 1812. After August 1814 the banks outside New England suspended, and quotations were recorded in bank paper whose discount varied by city. The 1814-era biographies accordingly decline to treat month-to-month movements in that window as market information.

Third, the Texas Indemnity Stock. Its quoted price reached 200 in December 1864. The bond was not twice as good as par; the currency in which it was quoted was half as good as coin, and the claim matured (in coin) on January 1, 1865. The quotation is the gold premium wearing a bond costume.

The general point is methodological. In this sample, the largest price movements are more often changes in the measuring rod than changes in the thing measured. Any statistic computed from these series — volatility, drawdown, yield — inherits the problem unless the unit of account is fixed first.

3. Prices set by regulation#

From 1863 to 1935 national banks could issue circulating notes against U.S. bonds deposited as collateral, and the tax and eligibility rules attached to that privilege priced several of the largest issues in this book. The 2 percent Consols of 1930 — $599.7 million outstanding for three decades — traded at or above par for years while carrying a coupon roughly half the contemporaneous market rate. Their price was not a forecast of anything; it capitalized a regulatory franchise. The biographies of the 4s of 1907 and the Ten-Twenties of 1898 show the same force at earlier dates: bonds held at premiums because banks needed them, with yields that measure note-issue economics rather than the price of time.

The Act of August 5, 1909 supplies the experiment. The 3 percent Panama Canal bonds of 1911 were issued without the circulation privilege — the first major issue sold on investment terms alone since the Civil War era. Through the stress of 1917–1921 the privileged Panama 2s never traded below roughly 96 while the unprivileged 3s fell to 80 and then to 74. Same obligor, same era, same gold clause; the entire gap is the regulatory subsidy. The episode deserves emphasis because the temptation to read pre-1917 U.S. bond yields as market interest rates is strong, and for the 2 percent family it is wrong. Macaulay made this point in 1938; the paired price series here let one see it plainly.

A corollary: when the Federal Reserve Act rendered the note-issue franchise obsolete, the propped prices sagged without any change in federal credit. The Consols’ price trough in October 1913 coincides with the Act’s passage. Deregulation, like regulation, moves prices that credit theory alone cannot explain.

4. The arithmetic of contract terms#

A recurring hazard in reading these series is to attribute to news what is due to contract. The biographies document several variants.

Pull to par. A bond approaching a fixed redemption date at par must converge to par. The declines of the 6% consols after 1875, of the 4½s of 1891 after 1882, and of the 8s of 1800 after 1802 are premium amortization, not distress. Chapters flag each case because automated event detection reliably misreads them.

Deferred coupons. The Deferred Sixes of 1790 paid nothing until 1801 and 6 percent thereafter. Their discount to the immediate Sixes — roughly \(42 per \)100 in 1790–92, $3 by 1800, zero from January 1801 — is time-value arithmetic observable in market data two centuries before the term “zero-coupon curve” existed. During the Quasi-War the deferred stock appreciated while the immediate stock fell: pull-to-conversion offset rising yields. No credit interpretation is needed or warranted.

Redemption caps. Hamilton’s Sixes were redeemable at no more than \(8 per \)100 per year, principal and interest inclusive. The cap converted them into quasi-annuities, supported above-par prices when rates fell, and obstructed Gallatin’s debt reduction — which is why the exchange operations of 1807 and 1812 exist. The two exchanges are mirror images: 1807 traded the cap away to permit faster redemption; 1812 traded it away to defer redemption twelve years, converting a mandatory amortization stream into a postwar bullet at the moment the Treasury was borrowing at 88. Liability management is not a modern invention.

Embedded options. The Liberty Loans carried conversion privileges that created five of this book’s securities. The privileges functioned as promised: when coupons escalated from 3½ to 4 to 4¼ percent, earlier subscribers could follow, and the quantity series record the migrations to the million. Two measurements deserve note. The converted and unconverted Second Liberty 4s and 4¼s traded roughly eight cents apart over 88 months — an open conversion window welds two prices together. And the Victory notes of 1919, issued as interconvertible twins (4¾ percent partially tax-exempt; 3¾ percent fully tax-exempt), show identical prices in 19 of 36 common months while the quantities sort monotonically toward the taxable coupon as rates and surtax expectations moved — clientele sorting revealed in quantities, with a break-even surtax near 21 percent implied by the coupon spread.

Price-guarantee clauses. The 1814 loans carried a most-favored-lender clause: if a later tranche sold cheaper, earlier subscribers were retroactively credited. The clause is why recorded sale prices of 88 and 80 coexist within one loan, and it is an early solution to a mechanism-design problem — sequential borrowing under deteriorating credit — that the biographies of 1813–1814 document in detail.

Three observations. First, the coupon a government must offer to sell at par is a credit spread measured at the source; the fall from 8 percent (1798) to 3 percent (1898) for long money is the reputational history of the United States in one series. Second, the distribution technology is itself informative. The government borrowed from three men in 1813, from a bank syndicate in 1895, and from eighteen million households in 1918; each shift followed political revulsion at the terms the previous technology extracted, and each shift was itself an investment in the fiscal franchise. Third, failures measure as well as successes: the two 1894 public offerings that could not hold the gold reserve, and the undersubscribed Ten-Forties of 1864, identify the limits of what the market would absorb at posted terms.

6. The credibility cycle, and its 1933 exception#

Arranged chronologically, the biographies repeat one plot. A war or crisis destroys the government’s capacity to borrow. A fiscal-monetary settlement follows — the Funding Act of 1790, the postwar consolidation of 1815–1821, the tariff-and-recovery settlement of 1843–1848, the Public Credit Act of 1869 with resumption in 1879, the Gold Standard Act of 1900. Prices then appreciate for years as the market gradually comes to believe the settlement. The appreciation is not instantaneous; the interval between the Public Credit Act (1869) and resumption (1879), during which the 6% consols climbed from roughly 108 toward gold parity, is a decade-long record of belief formation. Investors who bought Union debt in greenbacks and were repaid in gold earned the reward for correctly forecasting a regime; the biographies of the Five-Twenties make the magnitude explicit.

The cycle has one exception, and the book ends on it. In 1933 Congress abrogated the gold clause in outstanding federal bonds; the dollar was devalued from \(20.67 to \)35 per ounce; in Perry v. United States (1935) the Supreme Court held the abrogation unconstitutional as applied to federal obligations and simultaneously denied the bondholder any remedy. Holders of the Fourth Liberty Loan were repaid in dollars worth 41 percent less gold than their contract specified. Every previous settlement in the sample had resolved a crisis by honoring the letter of outstanding promises — sometimes at great cost, as in 1869–1879. The 1933–35 settlement resolved one by rewriting them. The price record registers the event only faintly (the called bonds were exchanged near par in current dollars), which is itself instructive: a default executed through the unit of account leaves little trace in prices denominated in that unit. Measurement problem (Section 2) and regime change (this section) are, in the limit, the same problem.

7. What the record does not say#

The tiering of chapters described in the introduction reflects a fact about the data that a synthesis should restate. Of 2,857 issues in the database, 77 have twelve or more monthly price observations. Prices are missing for identifiable economic reasons: floats locked in bank vaults for the circulation privilege, small tranches absorbed by conversion, notes held to maturity. Silence is therefore correlated with the very mechanisms Sections 3 and 4 describe, and inferences drawn only from the well-quoted issues overweight securities that traded freely. Several biographies also document artifacts — isolated prints, gap-spanning “events,” stale quotations repeated for months — and decline to narrate them. We flag this here because the collection is likely to be used as a data source: the chapters’ event tables record which detected movements we could attribute, which we could not, and which we judge to be defects of the record. Treating the third category as market history would manufacture findings.

8. Conclusion#

The biographies support a compact summary. United States bond prices from 1790 to 1935 are measurements, in shifting and sometimes corrupted units, of three things: the market’s belief in a sequence of fiscal-monetary settlements; the value of regulatory privileges attached to particular securities; and the arithmetic of the contracts themselves. Separating the three requires the statutory and historical detail that occupies most of each chapter; no spread or yield computed from the raw series does it automatically. Once separated, the residual — belief in the settlements — traces a government that spent a century and a half earning the right to borrow long at low rates, and that exercised, once, the sovereign’s option to redefine the unit in which its promises were kept. The reader may decide which of those two facts the prices in this book measure more precisely.
