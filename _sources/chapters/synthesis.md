# Synthesis: What the Prices Say

## 1. Object of this chapter

The forty-nine biographies in this book each study one security. This chapter studies
the collection. It assembles regularities that recur across the biographies and states
what the assembled price and quantity records do and do not establish. The evidence is
the Hall–Payne–Sargent database: monthly prices and quantities outstanding for U.S.
federal securities from 1790 to 1960, together with the statutory terms of each issue.
The method throughout has been to treat a bond price as a measurement — an opinion,
recorded at a date, about whether a government would keep a particular promise — and to
ask what moved the measurements.

Six findings organize the chapter. (i) A price cannot be read until its unit of account
is identified; for roughly a quarter of the sample the unit is not what it appears to
be. (ii) Several of the largest issues in the collection were priced by regulation
rather than by time preference, and one deliberate design change of 1909–1911 lets us
measure the difference. (iii) Much of what looks like price *drift* is the mechanical
arithmetic of embedded options and finite maturities, not news about credit. (iv) The
terms on which the Treasury could *sell* debt — primary-market prices and coupons —
measure reputation more sharply than secondary-market prices do. (v) The two-century
record is a repeated cycle: credit destroyed by war or panic, then rebuilt by a fiscal
settlement that the market learns to believe; the cycle ran intact until 1933, when the
government for the first time repriced an outstanding promise by statute. (vi) The
gaps and silences in the record are themselves data.

## 2. Units of account

A dollar price of a dollar claim is uninformative until one knows which dollar is
meant. Three episodes in the collection turn on this point.

First, the Civil War. After specie payments were suspended in December 1861, the
quotations in the database are greenback prices of bonds whose coupons — and, after
the Public Credit Act of 1869, principal — were payable in coin. The Loan of February
1861 traded at 106–114 in 1864; in the second half of that year the gold premium
exceeded 100 percent, and in gold units the bond stood near or below 50. The biographies of the wartime issues (the February 1861
loan, the Five-Twenties, the Ten-Forties) therefore read every wartime movement twice:
once as a statement about Union credit, once as a statement about the greenback. Most
of the drama belongs to the greenback. The March 1865 *fall* in quoted prices, which a
naive reading assigns to a credit event at the moment of Union victory, is the gold
premium collapsing.

Second, the War of 1812. After August 1814 the banks outside New England suspended,
and quotations were recorded in bank paper whose discount varied by city. The 1814-era
biographies accordingly decline to treat month-to-month movements in that window as
market information.

Third, the Texas Indemnity Stock. Its quoted price reached 200 in December 1864. The
bond was not twice as good as par; the currency in which it was quoted was half as
good as coin, and the claim matured (in coin) on January 1, 1865. The quotation is the
gold premium wearing a bond costume.

The general point is methodological. In this sample, the largest price movements are
more often changes in the measuring rod than changes in the thing measured. Any
statistic computed from these series — volatility, drawdown, yield — inherits the
problem unless the unit of account is fixed first.

## 3. Prices set by regulation

From 1863 to 1935 national banks could issue circulating notes against U.S. bonds
deposited as collateral, and the tax and eligibility rules attached to that privilege
priced several of the largest issues in this book. The 2 percent Consols of 1930 —
$599.7 million outstanding for three decades — traded at or above par for years while
carrying a coupon roughly half the contemporaneous market rate. Their price was not a
forecast of anything; it capitalized a regulatory franchise. The biographies of the 4s
of 1907 and the Ten-Twenties of 1898 show the same force at earlier dates: bonds held
at premiums because banks needed them, with yields that measure note-issue economics
rather than the price of time.

Two statutes supply the experiment. Section 39 of the Act of August 5, 1909 authorized
further Panama Canal bonds at up to 3 percent, and the Act of March 2, 1911 let the
Secretary make them unreceivable as security for national bank notes. The 3 percent
Panama Canal bonds of 1911 were therefore issued *without* the circulation privilege —
the first major issue sold on investment terms alone since the Civil War era. They sold
at 102.58, about 2.9 percent to 1961. Between 1911 and 1916 the 3s yielded about 0.8 to
1.0 percentage point more than the privileged Panama 2s: same obligor, same era, same
gold clause, and that gap is the cleanest measure of the regulatory subsidy. Through
the stress of 1917–1921 the 2s never traded below roughly 96 while the 3s fell to 80
and then to 74. That larger gap mixes the privilege with duration and call terms: the
3s were a non-callable fifty-year bond, while the 2s had passed their optional
redemption dates. The episode deserves emphasis because the temptation to read
pre-1917 U.S. bond yields as market interest rates is strong, and for the 2 percent
family it is wrong. Macaulay made this point in 1938; the paired price series here
let one see it plainly.

A corollary: when the Federal Reserve Act rendered the note-issue franchise obsolete,
the propped prices sagged without any change in federal credit. The Consols' price
trough in October 1913 coincides with the congressional endgame of the Act. Deregulation, like
regulation, moves prices that credit theory alone cannot explain.

## 4. The arithmetic of contract terms

A recurring hazard in reading these series is to attribute to news what is due to
contract. The biographies document several variants.

*Pull to par.* A bond approaching a fixed redemption date at par must converge to par.
The declines of the 6% consols after 1875, of the 4½s of 1891 after 1882, and of the
8s of 1800 after 1802 are premium amortization, not distress. Chapters flag each case
because automated event detection reliably misreads them.

*Deferred coupons.* The Deferred Sixes of 1790 paid nothing until 1801 and 6 percent
thereafter. Their discount to the immediate Sixes — roughly $42 per $100 in 1790–92,
$3 by 1800, zero from January 1801 — is time-value arithmetic observable in market
data two centuries before the term "zero-coupon curve" existed. During the Quasi-War
the deferred stock *appreciated* while the immediate stock fell: pull-to-conversion
offset rising yields. No credit interpretation is needed or warranted.

*Redemption caps.* Hamilton's Sixes were redeemable at no more than $8 per $100 per
year, principal and interest inclusive. The cap converted them into quasi-annuities,
supported above-par prices when rates fell, and obstructed Gallatin's debt reduction —
which is why the exchange operations of 1807 and 1812 exist. The two exchanges are
mirror images: 1807 traded the cap away to permit faster redemption; 1812 traded it
away to *defer* redemption twelve years, converting a mandatory amortization stream
into a postwar bullet a year before the Treasury had to borrow at 88. Liability
management is not a modern invention.

*Embedded options.* The Liberty Loans carried conversion privileges that created five
of this book's securities. The privileges functioned as promised: when coupons
escalated from 3½ to 4 to 4¼ percent, earlier subscribers could follow, and the
quantity series record the migrations to the million. Two measurements deserve note.
The converted and unconverted Second Liberty 4s and 4¼s traded roughly eight cents
apart over 88 months — an open conversion window welds two prices together. And the
Victory notes of 1919, issued as interconvertible twins (4¾ percent partially
tax-exempt; 3¾ percent fully tax-exempt), show identical prices in 19 of 36 common
months while the quantities sort monotonically toward the taxable coupon as rates and
surtax expectations moved — clientele sorting revealed in quantities, with a
break-even surtax near 21 percent implied by the coupon spread.

*Price-guarantee clauses.* The 1814 loans carried a most-favored-lender clause, written
into the Treasury's invitation for subscriptions rather than into the statute
(Bayley): if a later sale went cheaper, earlier subscribers were credited with
additional stock. The May 1814 loan sold at 88. A separate August 1814 offering of $6
million raised about $4.3 million for $5.4 million of stock, at 80; that sale triggered
the clause, and the May subscribers received supplemental stock. The clause is an
early solution to a mechanism-design problem — sequential borrowing under deteriorating
credit — that the biographies of 1813–1814 document in detail.

## 5. Primary markets as measurement

Secondary-market prices move for many reasons. The terms of a *new* sale confront the
government with a single number, and the sequence of such numbers across the sample
is a usable time series of reputation.

| Date | Operation | Terms |
|---|---|---|
| 1790 | Funding Act exchange | par, with coupon reduced ex post (the deferral and the 3s) |
| 1798–1800 | Quasi-War loans | 8 percent coupons to sell near par |
| 1813 | Sixteen Million Loan | 88, syndicate of three |
| 1814 | May loan; separate August offering | 88 in May; 80 in August, which triggered the guarantee clause |
| 1842 | Loan of 1842 | 6s at about par (Bayley: $8,343,886 sold for $8,301,468, about 99.5; the database records 97½), placed with difficulty |
| 1843 | Loan of 1843 | 5s at ~101 |
| 1848 | Loan of 1848 | 6s above 103 |
| 1861 | February loan | ~89, bids below minimum rejected |
| 1870–79 | Refunding | 6s replaced by 5s, 4½s, 4s at par |
| 1895 | Belmont–Morgan contract | 4s at 104½, reoffered by the syndicate at 112¼; market near 120 within weeks |
| 1896–98 | Popular loans | 1896 4s at ~111; 1898 3s at par, ~7× oversubscribed |
| 1917–19 | Liberty and Victory loans | 3½–4¾ percent to tens of millions of subscribers |

Three observations. First, the coupon a government must offer to sell at par is a
credit spread measured at the source; the fall from 8 percent (1798) to 3 percent
(1898) for long money is the reputational history of the United States in one series.
Second, the *distribution technology* is itself informative. The government borrowed
from three men in 1813, from a bank syndicate in 1895, and from eighteen million
households in 1918; each shift followed political revulsion at the terms the previous
technology extracted, and each shift was itself an investment in the fiscal franchise.
Third, failures measure as well as successes: the two 1894 public offerings that
could not hold the gold reserve, and the undersubscribed Ten-Forties of 1864,
identify the limits of what the market would absorb at posted terms.

## 6. The credibility cycle, and its 1933 exception

Arranged chronologically, the biographies repeat one plot. A war or crisis destroys
the government's capacity to borrow. A fiscal-monetary settlement follows — the
Funding Act of 1790, the postwar consolidation of 1815–1821, the tariff-and-recovery
settlement of 1843–1848, the Public Credit Act of 1869 with resumption in 1879, the
Gold Standard Act of 1900. Prices then appreciate for years as the market gradually
comes to believe the settlement. The appreciation is not instantaneous; the interval
between the Public Credit Act (1869) and resumption (1879), during which the 6%
consols climbed from roughly 108 toward gold parity, is a decade-long record of
belief formation. Investors who bought Union debt in greenbacks and were repaid in
gold earned the reward for correctly forecasting a regime. The Public Credit Act of
March 18, 1869 shows the settlement in prices: the average price of three Five-Twenty issues
(the Five-Twenties of 1865 and the Consols of 1865 and 1867), whose principal the Act
pledged to pay in coin, stood less than 1
point above the Ten-Forties in January 1869 and about 9 points above them in March and
April 1869.

The cycle has one exception, and the book ends on it. In 1933 Congress abrogated the
gold clause in outstanding federal bonds; the dollar was devalued from $20.67 to $35
per ounce; in *Perry v. United States* (1935) the Supreme Court held the abrogation
unconstitutional as applied to federal obligations and simultaneously denied the
bondholder any remedy. Holders of the Fourth Liberty Loan were repaid in dollars worth
41 percent less gold than their contract specified. Every previous settlement in the
sample had resolved a crisis by *honoring* the letter of outstanding promises —
sometimes at great cost, as in 1869–1879. The 1933–35 settlement resolved one by
rewriting them. The price record registers the event only faintly (the called bonds
were exchanged near par in current dollars), which is itself instructive: a default
executed through the unit of account leaves little trace in prices denominated in
that unit. Measurement problem (Section 2) and regime change (this section) are, in
the limit, the same problem.

## 7. What the record does not say

The tiering of chapters described in the introduction reflects a fact about the data
that a synthesis should restate. Of 2,857 issues in the database, 77 have twelve or
more monthly price observations. Prices are missing for identifiable economic
reasons: floats locked in bank vaults for the circulation privilege, small tranches
absorbed by conversion, notes held to maturity. Silence is therefore correlated with
the very mechanisms Sections 3 and 4 describe, and inferences drawn only from the
well-quoted issues overweight securities that traded freely. Several biographies also
document artifacts — isolated prints, gap-spanning "events," stale quotations repeated
for months — and decline to narrate them. Some bonds share one price series:
the Eight Per Cent Loans of 1798 and 1800 carry identical prices; the Loan of 1812 and
the Exchanged Six Per Cent Stock of 1812 carry identical prices, and the Sixteen
Million and Seven and One-Half Million Loans of 1813 share a series that matches them
in most months; the two 1814 loans carry identical prices; and from 1801 the Deferred
Sixes carry essentially the price of the Six Per Cent Stock. Comparisons between
those bonds measure nothing. We flag this here because the collection is
likely to be used as a data source: the chapters' event tables record which detected
movements we could attribute, which we could not, and which we judge to be defects of
the record. Treating the third category as market history would manufacture findings.

## 8. Conclusion

The biographies support a compact summary. United States bond prices from 1790 to 1935
are measurements, in shifting and sometimes corrupted units, of three things: the
market's belief in a sequence of fiscal-monetary settlements; the value of regulatory
privileges attached to particular securities; and the arithmetic of the contracts
themselves. Separating the three requires the statutory and historical detail that
occupies most of each chapter; no spread or yield computed from the raw series does it
automatically. Once separated, the residual — belief in the settlements — traces a
government that spent a century and a half earning the right to borrow long at low
rates, and that exercised, once, the sovereign's option to redefine the unit in which
its promises were kept. The reader may decide which of those two facts the prices in
this book measure more precisely.
