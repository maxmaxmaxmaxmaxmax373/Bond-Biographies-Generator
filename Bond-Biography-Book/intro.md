# Bond Biographies

## Lives of U.S. Federal Securities, 1790–1935

Every government bond is a promise, and every price is an opinion about whether that
promise will be kept. This book collects twenty-four *biographies* of individual U.S.
federal securities — each one tracing a single bond from the act of Congress that
authorized it, through its life in the market, to its redemption, conversion, or, in one
famous case, the rewriting of its terms by the government that issued it.

The biographies are built on the Hall–Payne–Sargent U.S. Federal Bond Database
(Payne, Szőke, Hall & Sargent, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2025), which assembles
prices, quantities, and terms for 2,857 federal debt issues from 1776 to 1960. Each
chapter interleaves historical narrative with charts and statistics computed directly
from that database, so the reader can see what the story claims *and* what the market
actually recorded.

## The arc of the book

Read in order, the chapters tell a single story in seven episodes:

**I. Hamilton's Funding System (1790–1800).** The Constitution's first great financial
act: the Funding Act of 1790 converts the chaotic Revolutionary debt into three
securities — the 6s, the deferred 6s, and the 3s — whose prices record the Panic of
1792, the birth of American securities markets, and the young republic's climb to
credit-worthiness. The Quasi-War 8s of 1800 show what that credit still cost when war
threatened.

**II. Jeffersonian Statecraft (1803–1807).** Debt as an instrument of policy: the
Louisiana Purchase paid for with 6% stock placed through Baring and Hope, and Gallatin's
1807 exchange operation, which re-engineered Hamilton's redemption terms so surpluses
could retire debt faster.

**III. The War of 1812 (1812–1815).** Federal credit's first great stress test. Five
bonds trace the descent — from the confident 1812 loan, through the 1813 syndicate
rescue at 88, to the 1814 borrowings at 80 amid a burned capital and suspended banks —
and the recovery, sealed by the 7% stock into which depreciated Treasury notes were
funded just as peace arrived.

**IV. Antebellum Crisis and the Mexican War (1842–1848).** Credit lost and regained:
a Treasury that could scarcely borrow in 1842, in the shadow of eight defaulting states,
sells the Loan of 1848 above par six years later — the first American war financed at
a premium.

**V. The Civil War (1861–1864).** The largest borrowing in the nation's history to that
point, told through three securities: the secession-crisis loan of February 1861, the
Five-Twenties that Jay Cooke sold to a mass public, and the Ten-Forties that tested —
and found — the limit of the market's terms. Wartime prices here are greenback prices;
reading them correctly is half the story.

**VI. Reconstruction, Refunding, and the Gold Standard (1862–1935).** The long
aftermath: the Currency Sixes that financed the Pacific Railroad, the great refunding
of the war debt into the 4s of 1907, and the 2% Consols of 1930 — a bond whose price
was set less by time preference than by regulation, and which died with the national
bank notes it backed.

**VII. The First World War and After (1917–1935).** The Liberty Loans create the modern
Treasury market and the statutory debt limit, teach a hard lesson about patriotic
pricing when they sink below par, and end in constitutional drama: the gold-clause
abrogation of 1933 and *Perry v. United States* — a right without a remedy.

## How to read the chapters

Each biography follows a common template: the bond's terms and authorizing act; the
historical context of its issue; its quantity outstanding over time; its price history
with detected significant events; volatility and yield analysis; comparisons with
related issues; and the story of its redemption. Charts are computed live from the
database. Where the record is thin — some issues have few or no surviving price
quotations — the chapters say so plainly rather than embroider.

## Sources and method

The quantitative backbone is the bond database; the narratives rest on standard works
of American financial history — Bayley's *National Loans of the United States*, Dewey's
*Financial History of the United States*, Studenski & Krooss, Friedman & Schwartz, and
era-specific classics cited chapter by chapter. Drafts were generated by the
[Bond Biographies Generator](https://github.com/maxmaxmaxmaxmaxmax373/Bond-Biographies-Generator)
toolkit and enriched with historical narrative; claims that could not be verified are
stated in deliberately general terms, and each chapter's references section points the
reader to the underlying scholarship.
